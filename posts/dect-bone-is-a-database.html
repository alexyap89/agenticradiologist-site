Bone is a database: field notes from a decade of DECT

By Alexander Yap · 12 Jul 2026

A dual-energy CT scanner is a machine that answers a question you had to think about first. That inversion is the whole lesson, and it took me a decade to learn it.

Here is the part of the physics that matters. We acquire two scans of the same patient at two different tube voltages. Every material attenuates those two beams in its own characteristic way, which means the scanner can decompose each voxel into basis materials — for our purposes, iodine and effective atomic number, one number per voxel. I grew up reading grayscale: a number of no particular kind, interpreted by context. DECT hands you a query result. Iodine concentration is a column you can select for, threshold, and plot.

The mental model that stuck: bone is a database. Each voxel is a row. Density, mineralisation, vascularity — columns you used to infer from context and guess at — are now selectable. In the 3D Imaging Lab we work with surgeons on pre-operative planning, and the change is not dramatic in the way the vendor slide suggests. It is quiet: the scan stops being an image and starts being a measurement we can put in the operative note. “Bone quality in the planned screw trajectory is low” is a sentence that used to require an essay of caveats. Now it requires a map.

Where the hype runs out

DECT is sold to clinicians as a miracle, and clinicians buy it because the demos are good. The demos are good because they show the easy 80%. The last 20% is where practice lives:

  • Precision is not accuracy. A map that looks smooth is not a map that is right. Cross-vendor calibration is a swamp, and the number on the colour scale means less than the pattern underneath it.
  • “Low bone quality” is a spectrum, not a binary. Every threshold you pick is a clinical decision wearing a lab coat. Say so out loud.
  • Noise floor matters when you are placing screws. The engineering question is not “can we produce a number” but “how much should we trust this number at this patient, this dose, this body habitus”.

Working with engineers on biomedical imaging taught me the vocabulary for all of this. They call it uncertainty quantification. We just call it not being wrong in front of a surgeon.

The transfer to AI

The reason this post exists is the transfer. I keep reapplying the same move to AI systems: stop admiring the output and start writing the query. What is the unit? What is the column? What is the noise floor? What is the honest threshold, and who owns it? An imaging AI that can’t answer those four questions is a demo. One that can is a measurement.

DECT taught me that the future of the scan is not “smarter pictures.” It is structure — pixels you can question. I increasingly suspect the future of medical AI is the same: not smarter captions, but systems where the evidence is queryable, the uncertainty is stated, and the person in the room owns the threshold.

Stop admiring the image. Start writing the query.
